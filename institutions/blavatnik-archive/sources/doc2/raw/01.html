About the Archive

The Blavatnik Archive is a nonprofit foundation dedicated to preserving and disseminating materials that contribute to the study of 20th-century world history, with a special focus on Jewish culture, World Wars I and II, and Soviet history. The Archive was founded in 2005 by the businessman and philanthropist Len Blavatnik to reflect his commitment to cultural heritage and expand his support for primary source–based scholarship and education.

Learn more

The collection began with the 2005 acquisition of 10,000 Judaica-themed postcards—images and messages from places and people that are now long gone.

Assembled over the course of decades by an anonymous collector, the postcards hailed from Western, Central, and Eastern Europe, North America, North Africa, and Palestine. Some portrayed scenes of daily life, offering photos and illustrations of synagogues, marketplaces, schools, and other community spaces. Others reflected more topical concerns, like holidays, contemporary personalities, or political and cultural events. Another subset dealt with the darker aspects of the Jewish experience, focusing on anti-Semitic tropes and depictions that circulated in the first half of the 20th century. 

Judaica Postcards

As we looked through the collection, we were struck by the unique insights offered by the postcards in their various functions—as art pieces, popular culture artifacts, personal correspondence, and historical testimony. These insights inspired our institutional goals: preserving materials that reflect the lived human experience of modern history; ensuring wide, open access to all primary source materials for scholars and the general public; and presenting these materials in a visually appealing format that encourages deeper appreciation and engagement.

After the initial acquisition, the Archive has substantially expanded the thematic scope of its holdings. Our collections tend to focus on medium types and subject areas that are not often found at other institutions, and which supplement and enhance existing holdings. Whenever possible, we aim to offer materials that present comprehensive frameworks for engaging with particular historical moments or topics.

One such topic that has become a major focus area for the Archive is the role and experience of Russian Jewish soldiers in the Second World War. In order to add their stories to the more familiar narratives about the Jewish experience of the war and the Holocaust, we launched a long-term project to record the testimonies of veterans who had fought in the Soviet armed forces and partisan detachments. Between 2006 and 2014, we visited nearly 1,200 veterans in 78 cities across 11 countries, recorded over 1,500 hours of video testimonies, and digitized more than 11,700 ephemera artifacts, including photographs, documents, letters, and diary pages.

Veteran Testimonies & Ephemera

Today, the Archive’s Veteran Testimonies & Ephemera project is the largest collection of materials dealing with the experience of Jewish soldiers in the Soviet armed forces during World War II.

Our collections continue to grow and evolve. Major additions were made with the 2014 acquisitions of 52,000 World War I postcards; 22,000 National Socialist Party ephemera; 5,000 World War II ephemera; and 1,300 Leningrad Siege postcards. In 2021, we acquired a Judaica and Holocaust collection with more than 5,500 items. Currently the Archive’s holdings consist of more than 145,000 items, including video testimonies, postcards, photographs, posters, drawings, illustrations, diaries, letters, state-issued documents, leaflets, periodicals, and books.

To ensure that our materials can reach a wide audience, we have prioritized free digital access, high-resolution reproductions, and seamless, user-oriented delivery. Our hope is that our holdings will encourage deeper engagement with and new perspectives on a variety of topics in modern history, and we have worked with scholars and instructors to facilitate research and incorporate our materials into classroom curricula. In addition to advancing academic research and education, the Archive aims to promote public interest and personal heritage discovery through dynamic website storytelling and non-digital programming, such as talks and exhibits that have taken place nationally and internationally at community centers, universities, galleries, and museums.

Testimonials

"Student emotions were aroused, especially by the interviews with the veterans and the Holocaust material," said Professor Zvi Gitelman, professor emeritus of political science and Preston Tisch Professor Emeritus of Judaic Studies, who implemented materials from the Archive in his course at the University of Michigan. "Statistics become names and the realities become all the more real," a student wrote him.

"The Blavatnik Foundation Archive contains unique materials that provide incredible insight on history and culture of Soviet Jews. Especially important are extensive interviews with the Soviet Jewish World War II veterans, transcribed and translated. This is an essential resource for scholars, researchers, and teachers. I cannot imagine studying history without it!"


—Anna Shternshis, Al and Malka Green Professor of Yiddish Studies and director of the Anne Tanenbaum Centre for Jewish Studies, University of Toronto

"Blavatnik Archive is a unique institution, an archive of the future that exists today. It's the place where the ages-old art of archiving—collecting, preserving, and cataloging—is taken to a new, creative level of transcribing, translating, and storytelling. Hence the archive creates meaning by turning the past into history."


—Professor Vassili Schedrin, Alfred and Isabel Bader Post-Doctoral Fellow in Jewish History and coordinator of the Russian and East European Studies Network, Queen’s University
