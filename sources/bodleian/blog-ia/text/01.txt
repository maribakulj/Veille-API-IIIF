 Implementing our AI Policy: hypothetical use cases
 Posted on 1 October 2026 by elizabethhatfield 
 The Bodleian Libraries has just published an AI policy , and it’s taken us a whole year of consultation to get it to a place where we feel it’s fit for purpose.
 It was really important to us to listen to our staff, students, academics and other libraries as we drafted this policy. When we set out, we were acutely aware that there were gaps in our knowledge and that there were risks we might not be able to foresee. 
 I’m proud that the policy we’re publishing in Michaelmas 2026 is vastly different from the draft principles the Libraries’ AI Strategy & Policy Group wrote in Michaelmas 2025. 
 One of the things that staff and students asked us for were tangible examples of how the policy could be used to guide real choices once it is published. We don’t have real examples yet as the policy is only just being published, but we have thought through some hypothetical scenarios and discussed these as part of the consultation.
 The purpose of these use cases is to show how we would like to implement our policy in a responsible and thoughtful manner.
 Library staff use case: Tom’s collection
 Tom works in a small cataloguing team facing a backlog of under-described pamphlets, all out of copyright. He has been given permission to trial an approved AI tool, a large language model (LLM) chatbot, to help produce first-draft summary descriptions of the pamphlets.
 Before starting, Tom checks the University’s approved tools list and confirms whether this task (to draft subject terms and summaries for out of copyright material) is a sensible and proportionate use of AI. Tom wants to avoid the habit of simply reaching for AI as a default when it may not be necessary.
 Policy principle applied: Adopt AI responsibly and proportionately 
 Tom runs each pamphlet’s scanned text through the tool, which suggests a description, subject terms and a short summary. Tom reads every suggestion before deciding whether to use it. Nothing is published automatically. Tom treats the AI output the way he would treat a trainee’s first draft; he finds the suggestions useful, but they require his professional judgement and need occasional edits and corrections.
 Policy principle applied: maintain human accountability 
 Part of the way through his task, Tom notices that the AI has used outdated and offensive terminology to describe one pamphlet’s subject matter. This is language that the Bodleian Libraries would not use today in descriptions, even though the term is present in the source text.
 Tom rewrites the offending text, drawing on the Libraries’ We Are Our History work and other library guidance on best practice around outdated terminology. He then flags the pattern to his team so that others can watch for the same issue. Where he is unsure whether wording could cause offence or misrepresent the material, he raises it with his manager, rather than guessing.
 Policy principle applied: advocate for lawful and ethical actions 
 When the batch of pamphlet scans is complete, Tom adds a short note to the project file. The note states that these records were drafted with AI assistance and reviewed by cataloguing staff before publication. Anyone looking at the collection later can see how the descriptions were produced.
 Policy principle applied: be transparent about AI use 
 Tom’s manager had earlier made sure that Tom had time in his diary to learn the tool properly. This included attending AI training delivered by the AI Competency Centre and an iSkills AI workshop . His manager had also given Tom the opportunity to ask questions, which helped him feel able to learn and experiment safely and to say when something didn’t work.
 Policy principle applied: support informed and critical AI use 
 Student use case: Jenny’s search
 Jenny is a second-year undergraduate historian starting a new essay. She logs into SOLO to find sources about early modern print culture. Jenny notices a toggle for “AI-assisted search”, which is clearly labelled and is switched off by default.
 Policy principle applied: be transparent about AI use 
 Jenny is environmentally conscious and wants to understand whether her use of the search assistant would be proportionate. She reads a short note on the webpage that explains what the search assistant does and that ordinary search still works exactly as before if she chooses not to use it. It also tells her the carbon impact of each AI-assisted search compared to ordinary search.
 Policy principle applied: minimise AI’s environmental impact 
 Curious, Jenny switches on the search assistant. Jenny checks the short help note linked from the results page, which explains what is being searched, SOLO records, some licensed databases and the Bodleian’s digitised collections, and confirms that the open internet is not included. This helps her judge how much to trust what she sees, so she knows how best to apply the information to her work.
 Policy principle applied: protect and enhance our collections 
 The tool summarises a cluster of results and suggests some related open-access items that Jenny had not found herself. Underneath the summary, a note reminds her that this is a starting point, not a citable source, and that she should read and cite the original material directly.
 Policy principle applied: advocate for lawful and ethical actions 
 One suggested source turns out not to exist. Jenny uses a “report a problem” link next to the result and finds a page explaining who reviews these reports and roughly how long it takes to fix things.
 Policy principle applied: maintain human accountability 
 Jenny finishes her search having found genuinely useful material, understanding clearly which parts came from AI assistance and which she found and read herself. Keen to use AI well in the run-up to her exams, but without time to attend a full training course, she follows a link to the Bodleian’s guide on AI for Literature Searching . It’s focused specifically on the literature searching process, covering how to use AI in a search, how to evaluate AI-generated sources, how to reference AI in her work, and where the University’s policies on AI sit, so within a few minutes she has exactly what she needs for the task in front of her.
 Policy principle applied: support informed and critical AI use 
 Researcher use case: Dr Amara’s dataset 
 Dr Amara, a computational linguist and postdoctoral researcher, wants to study whether AI models trained only on women authors sound different from models trained only on men, and whether readers can tell the difference. To do this, she needs to build a large training dataset from openly available texts, including material held in the Bodleian’s institutional repository, the Oxford Research Archive (ORA), where Oxford researchers’ publications and theses are made freely available online. As a new researcher to Oxford, Dr Amara reads the University’s Policy for Using Generative AI in Research .
 Dr Amara has read about some cultural heritage organisations suffering from heavy web scraping. So rather than writing a script to bulk-download material directly, which could slow the repository for other users or breach terms of use, Dr Amara contacts the Bodleian Libraries first to discuss what she needs.
 Policy principle applied: support responsible openness 
 A member of staff at the Bodleian Libraries responds to Dr Amara, talking her through a route that will give her the scale of access her research requires while avoiding the unrestricted, unannounced harvesting that has caused problems for the repository in the past. (This includes AI bots that ignore normal usage limits.) Dr Amara is delighted to learn more about how ORA works and is confident that her new route will give her the data she needs. She can now describe her data collection methodology accurately and with integrity. 
 Policy principle applied: protect and enhance our collections 
 In return, Amara agrees to cite her use of ORA in any resulting publications and confirms she will not redistribute the underlying texts themselves. In line with good practice and the requirements of copyright law, the resulting model, rather than the source material, will be made openly available. This will enable other researchers to build on Dr Amara’s work without needing separate access to the original texts.
 Policy principle applied: advocate for lawful and ethical actions 
 Because the Bodleian Libraries chose to have this conversation rather than simply blocking large-scale access outright, Dr Amara’s research proceeds on a timeline that works for her. Bodleian Libraries’ staff also retain confidence that access to repository content at scale is being given responsibly and in line with the needs of readers.
 Policy principle applied: adopt AI responsibly and proportionately 
 What’s next
 As we think about and test AI applications in the Libraries, we will make sure we are transparent with our decisions and outcomes.
 We would love to hear from you about other use cases of AI, and how you are putting the policy into practice either at Oxford or within your own library. 
 Please send any feedback or comments to:  strategy@bodleian.ox.ac.uk . 
 Comments will be reviewed on a termly basis by the Libraries’ AI Strategy & Policy Group, which is responsible for updating the AI policy at least once a year.
